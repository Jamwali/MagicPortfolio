Most portfolio projects get compressed into the same shape: a title, a list of tools, and a screenshot. That format is quick to scan, but it hides the part I care about most—the chain of decisions that turns an idea into a dependable system.

This site treats presentation as another engineering problem. The animated project scenes are not decorative thumbnails. Each one abstracts the actual work: a scan entering a classifier, documents becoming embeddings, models moving through a benchmark, or interview notes becoming an information architecture.

## Motion should explain something

The project artwork is tied to scroll position instead of an automatic loop. Moving forward reveals the pipeline; moving back rewinds it. That gives the reader control and makes the motion behave like an explanation rather than an interruption.

It also means the experience needs a static answer. If someone prefers reduced motion, the scene resolves immediately instead of withholding information.

## Recruiters need two speeds

A useful portfolio has to work in a thirty-second scan and a ten-minute review. The home page carries the fast evidence: role, outcome, stack, and one memorable visual. The case studies hold the slower evidence: constraints, decisions, caveats, and what I would do next.

That second layer matters. A metric tells you what happened. A limitation tells you whether the person presenting it understands what the metric cannot prove.

## The standard I am aiming for

The site should make three things clear without asking the reader to infer them:

- what I personally worked on;
- what changed because of the work;
- where the evidence stops.

That is the same standard I want in the products I build: expressive on the surface, rigorous underneath, and honest about the boundary between the two.
